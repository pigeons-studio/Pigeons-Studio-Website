MANUAL · ENGLISH VERSION

ORCA//GLYPH

A live coding language in volume. Every letter is an operation, every operation is a glyph, and the Orca grid becomes a building of floors that you code with the keyboard and watch in 3D. This manual starts from zero: you don't need to know Orca to begin.

In ORCA//GLYPH you type characters on the keyboard, and each one appears on screen as a glyph. Throughout this manual, examples show both: the glyph that appears, and underneath, small, the key to type. In the text, reads "type D, see this glyph". The full table is in step 02.
operator MIDI / OSC output floor operator bang value cell written by the operator

01What ORCA//GLYPH is

A sequencer you write with letters. It makes no sound itself: it drives your instruments.

ORCA//GLYPH descends from Orca, the esoteric programming language created by Hundred Rabbits. The principle is the same: on a grid, every letter of the alphabet is an operation. Operations read each other, add up, trigger one another, and end up sending MIDI notes, OSC or UDP messages to your synths, to Ableton, VCV Rack, SuperCollider or a Monome Norns.

What ORCA//GLYPH adds:

  • Floors. The Orca grid is stacked on several levels, and six operators carry values and bangs from one floor to another.
  • Glyphs. Every character is a glyph of the As-Simt alphabet, extruded in 3D. Every uppercase operator is drawn with the sign of its poetic definition: T, the sequencer, is "weave".
  • A view in volume. You code with the keyboard as in Orca, and you walk around the code, through it, turn it into a point cloud or a glitch.
  • A score. What is played can be written as a score, for musicians to play it again.
A classic .orca file opens in ORCA//GLYPH on floor 0, and an ORCA//GLYPH session opens in Orca. Everything you learn here also works in Orca.

03The grid and time

A grid of cells, and a clock that moves one frame at a time.

When it opens, the grid is empty: dots on a black background. The cursor is the golden pigeon. Move it with the arrow keys, type a character to write it in the cell, Backspace to erase it.

Time moves in frames. At every frame, ORCA//GLYPH reads the whole grid, left to right and top to bottom, and runs each operator once. The frame counter is shown at the bottom of the screen, with the tempo.

  • Space starts or stops the clock. When stopped, the pigeon blinks.
  • Ctrl+F moves forward a single frame: handy to understand what is going on.
  • > and < raise and lower the tempo (in BPM). By default, 4 frames make one beat.

04Write an operator

Uppercase: it acts on every frame. Lowercase: it waits to be triggered.

Type 1A2: A adds the value on its left to the value on its right, and writes the result right below itself. After one frame, a 3 appears.

ADD
A is swarm in the language of images: a swarm grows by adding up. Change the 2 into a 5: the output becomes 6 on the next frame.

Uppercase, an operator acts on every frame. Lowercase (a), the same operator does nothing until it receives a bang (see step 06). It is the most important rule of the language.

In ORCA//GLYPH, uppercase letters are drawn with their poetic sign, and lowercase letters keep the ordinary alphabet: you see at once what runs all the time and what waits.

05Ports

Settings on the left, inputs on the right, output below.

Each operator reads cells around it, its ports:

  • on the left, the settings (also called "haste"): they are read before the operator acts, for example the speed of a clock;
  • on the right, the inputs: the values it works on;
  • below, the output: the cell where it writes.

An empty cell (.) counts as 0, or as the port's default value. When the pigeon is on an operator, the NAMES option shows what each port does, for example rate 4 · mod 8 → 3 for a clock.

A CLOCK
C (beat) counts from 0 to 7 then starts again: the 8 on the right is the modulo, the 4 on the left says it moves one step every 4 frames. The output changes before your eyes.

One operator's outputs become another one's inputs: just place the second one right next to it. All of Orca is made of these chains.

06The bang

A * that appears for one frame and triggers its neighbours.

The bang is a one-frame signal: a * that appears, wakes up the operators touching it (above, below, left, right), then disappears. It is what triggers lowercase operators and outputs.

A REGULAR BANG
D (rain) drops a bang below itself every 4 frames, like a drop falling in rhythm. With 2D4, it drops one every 8 frames (2 × 4).

Other operators make bangs too: F when two values are equal, U in a Euclidean rhythm, and E, N, S, W when they bump into something. You can also write a * by hand, or trigger the operator under the pigeon with Ctrl+P.

07Play a note

The : sends a MIDI note when it gets a bang.

The : reads five cells on its right: channel, octave, note, then, optionally, velocity and length. Place it next to the bang of a D:

ONE NOTE EVERY 4 FRAMES
:03C plays a C, at octave 3, on channel 1 (channels start at 0). Notes are written C D E F G A B; a lowercase letter is the sharp: c is C sharp.
  • Velocity: from 0 to g (16 = 127). Empty means f, a strong hit.
  • Length: how many frames the note is held. Empty means one frame.
  • % does the same, but monophonic: each new note cuts the previous one, ideal for analog synths that don't like chords.
No sound? ORCA//GLYPH is not a synth. Pick an instrument in OUTPUTS ▸ MIDI (see step 13), then the TEST button plays a C to check.

08Counting in base 36

A cell holds a single character: letters are numbers too.

Orca counts from 0 to z: 0–9 are 0 to 9, then a is 10, b 11… up to z, 35. So Do drops a bang every 24 frames. The full table is in the reference.

A result above 35 wraps back to 0: zA1 gives 0. That is what makes clocks and counters loop.

09A melody

T, weave, reads one value out of a row: it is the sequencer.

T has two settings on its left: the position (the key) and the length of the row to read. It writes below itself the value found at that position. Give it a clock as position, and it runs along the row like a shuttle on a loom.

EIGHT NOTES IN A LOOP
1C8 counts from 0 to 7 and writes the count in the marked cell, which is T's position.
8T therefore reads the eight notes CDEGAcdg one by one and writes the current note below itself.
1D2 drops a bang every 2 frames, next to :14 (channel 2, octave 4), whose third cell is precisely T's output.

Change the letters of the row while it plays: the melody changes on the fly. That is the whole spirit of live coding.

10A rhythm

U, circle, spreads hits over a cycle.

3 HITS OVER 8
3U8 gives a Euclidean rhythm: 3 bangs spread as evenly as possible over 8 steps. Try 5U8, 3U4, 7Ug. On a drum channel, two or three stacked U are enough for a groove.

To slow a rhythm down, put a clock on the left of the D or the U; to shift it, use H (silence), which locks the cell below it, or F (double) to keep only some steps.

11Variables

V, I carry it within me, keeps a value under a name.

WRITE THEN READ
aV5 stores 5 in variable a. Further on, Va (nothing on its left) reads variable a and writes 5 below itself. Variables are shared across the whole grid, and across every floor.

K (flock) reads several variables at once: 3Kabc writes the values of a, b and c below their names. Since the grid is read top to bottom, a variable written at the top can be rewritten lower down in the same frame: order matters.

12Floors

Several grids stacked up, and six operators to go from one to another.

All Orca operators act within their own floor. Page ↑ and Page ↓ move the pigeon from one floor to another; Ctrl+Shift+Page ↑ adds a floor. Floor 0 is the ground floor. Floors above the pigeon get darker, those below stay bright.

On a French AZERTY keyboard, the six floor operators are typed without Shift. In front of them, L is a floor number; empty, it means the floor just above.

13Connect the sound

ORCA//GLYPH speaks MIDI, OSC and UDP. Everything is set in the OUTPUTS glyph of the menu (the signal sign).

MIDI

Pick the instrument that receives the notes of : and %, the controls of ! and the pitch bend of ?. A connected instrument is recognised by itself within two seconds, and found again by its name at the next launch. Ctrl+. switches to the next output.

  • To software on the same computer (Ableton, Bitwig, VCV Rack): you need a virtual MIDI cable. On Windows, loopMIDI; on Mac, the IAC bus (Audio MIDI Setup); on Linux, ALSA or JACK.
  • In Ableton Live: a MIDI instrument track, input set to the virtual cable, and the In button on.
  • In VCV Rack: a MIDI-CV module set to the virtual cable, then :03c88 to send a note and a gate.
  • A USB synth shows up directly. On Windows, Bluetooth MIDI devices are not seen: use a USB cable or a virtual port.

CLOCK lets you follow the MIDI clock of another machine; otherwise, the internal clock is used.

OSC

= sends an OSC message when banged: the first character is the path, the following ones are sent as numbers. =a123 sends 1 2 3 to /a. Set the address and port in OUTPUTS: for example 57120 for SuperCollider, 4560 for Sonic Pi, 10111 for the Norns, or the IP address of another machine. Orca's default port is 49162.

UDP

; sends the text written on its right, by default to port 49161 of the computer. ;03Cff plays a note in Pilot, the small Hundred Rabbits synth that works with no setup.

Controls and pitch bend

  • ! (channel, control, value) sends a CC between 0 and 127, scaled from 36: !008 sends 28 on the first control (CC 64 by default, adjustable with the cc:0 command).
  • ? (channel, lsb, msb) sends a pitch bend.
  • The pg:channel;msb;lsb;program command changes the sound (program change, bank select).

The TEST button sends a C on channel 1 and /orcaglyph/test 1 over OSC: the easiest way to check a connection.

14Looking at the volume

The code is a place: you walk around it, you step inside.

Mouse and cameras

  • Left click: puts the pigeon on the cell you point at, on any visible floor; drag to select.
  • Right click drag: orbit. Wheel: zoom. Shift + right click: pan.
  • F3: next camera (3/4, top, front, orbit, AUTO). In AUTO, the camera slowly drifts around the pigeon; the wheel zooms while it drifts.
  • F2: the walk, first person (ZQSD or WASD, Space up, C down, Shift faster). Home reframes.

2D and 3D

Ctrl+D flattens the volume into a 2D Orca seen from above: the camera pulls back while narrowing its angle, the other floors fold onto the pigeon's floor. You code exactly as in Orca. The same shortcut unfolds the floors again.

Visions

Tab moves from one vision to the next: NORMAL (solid glyphs), CLOUD (Kinect-like points, colour gives depth), DASHES, EDGES, GLITCH (the 3D torn into pieces). F7 fires a glitch burst. The - operator triggers a local glitch from the code.

Layout

  • F8: PLAN (floors stacked, glyphs lying flat) or BUILDINGS (the plan rises like a drawbridge, each column becomes a tower).
  • F4: the cut, hiding the floors above the pigeon.
  • F10: one texture per floor (squares, dashes, dots), to read stacked floors.
  • F9: links. When several floors bang in the same frame, a lightning of pixels joins the bangs from floor to floor.
  • F11: full screen. F6: perf mode, only the 3D is left.

Tracking and names

TRACKING puts a small reticle on every bang, with what it triggers (MIDI C3, RELAY…). NAMES shows at the pigeon what the touched cell does: an operator's name and values, the setting of an input cell, the value of a base-36 number.

15The glyph menu

Ten glyphs standing at the edge of the floors. At rest they are signs; open, they show their settings.

Near the mouse, a glyph lights up. A click opens it: it lies flat in front of you, and its settings appear, engraved in pixels. A toggle has its pixel in front of the word; a value is set with the wheel, or by clicking its left half (−) or right half (+). Esc closes. The "–" button tucks the whole menu into the golden pigeon.

16The score

What is played gets written down, for musicians to play it again.

  1. Open the panel with F5 (or the song glyph).
  2. For each MIDI channel played, give an instrument name and a type: high melodic (treble clef), low melodic (bass clef), percussion, or auto (channel 10 is the drums).
  3. Start recording: Ctrl+F5, the ● REC button, or $rec from the grid. Play. Stop the same way.
  4. Read the take back as STAVES (the score) or PHRASES (each note said in the language of images).

Each part has two lines: above, the glyphs of the playing mode (song, drone, whisper, cry, click, heartbeat); below, the notes and their dynamics. Time is counted like this: 1 frame = a sixteenth note, 4 frames = one beat, 16 frames = one bar of 4/4. Percussion is written on a drum staff, each element in its place.

Sharing

  • SVG EXPORT (to print), MUSICXML (for MuseScore, Sibelius or Dorico), TEXT (the phrases).
  • PAGE: a single .html file with the instrumentation, the reading key, the score and the phrases.
  • SHARE: a folder ready to send, with a PDF for WhatsApp and 1080 × 1350 images for an Instagram carousel.
  • STAND: the score page by page, large, turned with the keyboard or a pedal.
  • VIDEO: the score scrolling at tempo, as a vertical video.

The recorder listens to what goes out to MIDI without changing the sound.

17Sessions and files

  • While you play, the grid saves itself. The software starts on an empty grid; the previous session reopens with SESSIONS ▸ LAST SESSION.
  • Ctrl+S saves the grid under the typed name, or under the date and time. The list in the SESSIONS panel reloads any session.
  • Before every load, the current grid is kept: nothing is lost by loading by mistake.
  • Sessions are .orca files: the floors from bottom to top, separated by an empty line. They also open in Orca.
  • Ctrl+Shift+D loads the demo patch, on four floors: RHYTHM, ECHO, MELODY, SKY. Read it from bottom to top: it is the best follow-up to this manual.

22Lineage and links

ORCA//GLYPH extends Orca by Hundred Rabbits, an esoteric programming language under the MIT licence, and reuses its engine (Orca Ts): operators, clock, MIDI, OSC and UDP work as in Orca. The glyphs come from the As-Simt alphabet, shared with ARCHI//SIMT and OPI//NET; no sign was invented for the occasion, each one is chosen for its meaning.

The project grew out of a reflection on the creolisation of computer language, begun during Koo Des's residency at Villa Albertine in New Orleans: writing code with signs that belong to us.
